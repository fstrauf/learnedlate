# Obsidian and Cursor for Better Writing: A Step-by-Step Workflow

Obsidian stores your notes as plain markdown. Cursor is an AI editor that can read those files as context while you draft. Used together, they turn a personal knowledge base into a writing system: less blank-page friction, more continuity with what you already know, and drafts that sound like you instead of a generic model.

I run this setup for long-form posts and technical articles. The workflow below is the practical version — setup, daily loop, and the failure modes that waste time.

## Why combine Obsidian and Cursor?

Most AI writing fails in one of two ways: the model invents structure you did not ask for, or it ignores the details you already researched. Obsidian is where those details live — quotes, outlines, meeting notes, half-finished arguments. Cursor is where you turn that material into a shippable draft without copy-pasting fragments into a chat window.

The combination works because:

- **Your notes stay the source of truth.** AI suggests; you decide.
- **Context is local and multi-file.** You can point the model at a folder, not one pasted blob.
- **Markdown stays portable.** No proprietary lock-in between tools.
- **Editing is visual.** Diff-style suggestions make it easier to accept only the parts that improve the draft.

If you only need light completions inside a single note, a vault plugin can be enough. When you are restructuring an article, merging research, or rewriting for clarity across sections, Cursor earns its place.

## Setting up the workflow

### 1. Keep Obsidian as the vault home

Continue capturing ideas, research, and outlines in Obsidian as usual. Prefer short atomic notes for facts and longer “draft” notes for posts in progress. Consistent filenames and folders help Cursor (and you) find material later.

### 2. Open the vault in Cursor

In Cursor, open the same folder Obsidian uses as the vault root. You now have one filesystem both tools share. Leave Obsidian open for graph/search if you like; use Cursor when you are in drafting or rewrite mode.

### 3. Ground the AI in the right notes

Before asking for a full draft, attach or reference:

- The outline or brief
- Supporting research notes
- Any prior draft of the same piece
- Style or voice notes if you keep them

A short instruction beats a vague “write an article.” Example: “Rewrite the intro to 50 words, keep the thesis from `outline.md`, and do not invent product claims.”

### 4. Draft in markdown, review in diffs

Ask Cursor to expand sections one at a time rather than generating the entire post in one shot. Review every suggestion. Reject fluent but empty paragraphs. Keep examples and numbers that came from your notes.

### 5. Return the finished draft to your publish path

When the MDX or markdown is ready, commit or publish through your normal site pipeline. Optionally paste a final summary back into Obsidian so the vault stays current.

## Practical AI writing use cases

### Summarize research before you draft

Select a set of notes and ask for a bullet synthesis: claims, open questions, and contradictions. Use that as the spine of the outline instead of starting from a model’s generic structure.

### Turn speaking into writing

If you dictate or dump rough voice notes into the vault, use Cursor to clean structure while preserving phrasing that sounds like you. Pair this with [Speaking vs Writing](/blog/2025-02-10-speaking-vs-writing) if you are deciding which medium fits the idea first.

### Edit for clarity without flattening voice

Prompt for “cut filler, keep first-person examples” rather than “make this more professional.” Professional-by-default prompts often erase the operator detail that makes a post useful.

### Build social and newsletter variants

Once the long piece is solid, ask for a short LinkedIn post or email intro *from the same file*. That keeps messaging aligned and avoids rewriting from memory.

### Compare tools in context

When evaluating other assistants, keep the same vault workflow so comparisons are fair. For a broader stack view, see [Best AI Tools for Business 2025](/blog/best-ai-tools-for-business-2025) and treat this Obsidian + Cursor loop as the writing-specific path.

## A simple daily loop

1. **Capture** in Obsidian during the day (links, quotes, half thoughts).
2. **Cluster** once: move related notes under a draft folder or link them.
3. **Open the draft folder in Cursor** when you have a 30–60 minute block.
4. **Expand one section**, review, commit mentally (or in git).
5. **Stop with a next-action note** at the top of the file so tomorrow’s session starts warm.

Protect deep work time the same way you would for non-AI writing. Tools do not replace focus — see [Make Space for Your Best Work](/blog/2025-03-05-make-space-for-your-best-work) if calendar noise is the real bottleneck.

## Common mistakes

- **Empty section headings.** Do not leave H2s with no body; models and readers both bounce.
- **One-shot full articles.** Quality drops and voice washes out. Section-by-section wins.
- **No source notes attached.** You get confident prose with weak grounding.
- **Duplicating the Substack version poorly.** If a longer post lives on Substack, the site version should be a complete, self-contained guide — not a teaser with broken related-link soup.
- **Ignoring privacy settings.** Treat client and strategy notes carefully when models send context to the cloud.

## What's next

Once the basic loop feels natural, layer in:

- Templates for briefs and outlines in the vault
- A fixed “voice” note you attach to every rewrite
- Light automation only after the manual workflow is stable — random AI agents usually add noise before they add speed

Obsidian remains the memory. Cursor remains the focused editor. Together they are less about “AI writing for you” and more about **writing with your own archive in the room**.
